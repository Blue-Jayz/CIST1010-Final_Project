My Story: The All-Nighter That Changed Everything

High school taught me I could get away with doing things at the last second. My first month at UNO proved that college does not work that way.

The Midnight Calculus Trap

Coming into UNO, I felt confident. I had handled rigorous classes in high school, so when my first major Calculus problem set was assigned, I figured I would just sit down and knock it out the evening before the deadline.

That was a huge mistake.

The assignment was not a quick worksheet; it was roughly 30 complex mathematical problems requiring deep conceptual understanding, multi-step algebraic manipulation, and graph checks. I started working around 8:00 PM thinking I would wrap up by 10:00 PM. By midnight, I was only halfway through. My eyes burned, my brain was running on empty, and panic set in. I kept grinding until well past 2:00 AM.

Technically, I submitted the assignment on time. But the cost was catastrophic: I got barely three hours of fractured sleep, showed up to class the next morning like a zombie, and felt physically exhausted for three straight days. That all-nighter threw my entire weekly rhythm into chaos.

The Ah-Ha Moment

When the fatigue wore off, I realized something so simple yet profound: the homework had about 30 questions, and the professor had given us nearly two full weeks to complete it.

Doing 30 brutal questions in one sleepless four-hour panic is miserable. But doing 3 to 5 questions a day over seven days takes only 25 minutes of focused effort per afternoon. Spreading it out gives your brain time to absorb concepts, sleep on tricky proofs, and ask the professor questions before the submission portal closes.

Where I Learned This (Lesson 1) Source: Personal Experience

Specific Source: My Calculus homework all-nighter in my first semester — MATH1930-856: Calculus For The Managerial, Life, And Social Sciences.
What it taught me: It cost me valuable sleep, and the homework had about 30 questions with plenty of time to spread them out rather than cramming them into one late night.

Why This Matters Cognitive Stamina & Sustainable Growth

This realization completely transformed my academic mindset from reactive survival to deliberate preparation. Complex problem-solving requires incubation: when you hit a mental roadblock on problem 14, stepping away allows your subconscious mind to work through the logic while you commute or sleep. Cramming denies your brain that synthesis window, turns learning into sheer anxiety, and leaves you unable to recall concepts during exams.

The Commuter Schedule: 40 Minutes, Gym, and Work

Unlike students who live in Scott Hall or University Village and can roll out of bed ten minutes before lecture, I have a 30 to 40 minute commute to UNO each way. If I leave home ten minutes late, I get trapped in peak Omaha morning traffic, fight for parking in the Pacific Street garage, and arrive stressed.

To keep my life together, I had to architect a disciplined routine:

  • Early Morning Gym Sessions: Waking up early enough to hit the gym, get a shower, and drive to campus before rush hour peaks. Hitting the gym gives me physical energy and resets my posture from sitting over screens.
  • Balancing a Job: Working shifts on top of 15 credit hours means free time does not happen accidentally. Every hour between classes has to be utilized on campus.
  • Overcoming Social Hesitation: As a commuter who leaves campus after class, starting conversations with new people was not easy. It felt tempting to just put headphones on and walk to my car, but I learned that staying isolated hurts your college experience.

Success in Computer Science II (CSCI 1620)

Heading into Computer Science II (CSCI 1620), I felt genuine imposter syndrome. I had not touched Python in about a year, and 1620 is known as a rigorous weed-out course in the College of IS&T that moves quickly into object-oriented programming, data structures, and algorithmic complexity.

Instead of repeating my Calculus mistake, I applied deliberate practice immediately:

  • I locked in on studying from day one, reviewing syntax and building small test scripts right after class.
  • Rather than just reading lecture slides passively, I actively coded along in an IDE, purposely breaking functions to see the stack traces.
  • I treated weekly programming labs as opportunities to master concepts instead of chores to finish.

The result? I excelled in CSCI 1620. Locking in on studying works, and actively practicing what you learn in class makes the concepts stick permanently.

Where I Learned This (Lesson 8) Source: Personal Experience & Class Assignments

Specific Source: Computer Science II (CSCI 1620).
What it taught me: Practicing what you learn in class gave me a much deeper understanding of the material so I could do well on tests and labs and be able to use it in real-world software scenarios.

Why This Matters Active Learning vs. Passive Illusion

In technical fields like computer science and engineering, reading about code is not the same as writing code. When you actively practice through labs and personal experiments, you train muscle memory and error-debugging resilience that cannot be acquired from slides alone. This practice-first habit gave me the genuine technical confidence that later helped me stand out in professional internship interviews.

Reflect on Your Own Honest Self-Check for Incoming Students

"What is your biggest high school study habit that you know will not survive the freedom and pace of college?"

In high school, teachers give daily reminders and coursework is largely structured for you. In college, syllabi are posted once, and nobody is going to tap your shoulder to tell you an exam is in 72 hours.

Look at your current week: do you have a single assignment you are currently planning to do the night before it is due? What would happen if you did half of it today? Test it once and feel the difference in your evening stress levels.
