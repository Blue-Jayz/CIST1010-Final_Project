My Story: The All-Nighter That Changed Everything

Step 1 in Class Walkthrough • Going from high school cramming to actually planning ahead in college.

The Midnight Calculus Trap

Coming into UNO, I felt pretty confident. I'd handled college classes in high school, so when my first big Calculus problem set showed up, I figured I'd just sit down and knock it out the evening before it was due.

That was a mistake.

The assignment wasn't a quick worksheet; it was roughly 30 tough math problems with multi-step algebra, graphing, and several parts per problem. I sat down at 10:00 PM after work thinking I'd wrap up by midnight. By then, I was barely halfway done. My eyes burned, my brain was running on fumes, and panic started kicking in. I kept solving problem after problem until well past 2:00 AM.

I submitted the assignment late, losing one-tenth of a point per late question. Even worse, I got barely four hours of sleep, showed up to class the next day like a zombie, and felt physically exhausted for three straight days. That one all-nighter threw off my whole week.

The Ah-Ha Moment

Once the exhaustion wore off, I realized something simple: the homework had about 30 questions, and the professor gave us nearly two full weeks to finish it.

Doing 30 tough questions in one sleepless four-hour panic is miserable. Doing 3 to 5 questions a day over seven days takes only about 25 minutes of effort each afternoon. Spreading it out gives your brain time to actually learn the concepts, sleep on tricky problems, and ask the instructor questions before the deadline closes.

Where I Learned This (Lesson 1) Source: Personal Experience

Specific Source: My Calculus homework all-nighter in my first semester (MATH1930-856: Calculus For The Managerial, Life, And Social Sciences).
What it taught me: Cramming cost me valuable sleep, and the homework had about 30 questions with plenty of time to spread them out instead of doing it all in one late night.

Why This Matters Brainpower & Better Habits

That night completely changed how I handle schoolwork, shifting me from last-minute panic to actually planning ahead. Hard math problems take time to sink in: when you get stuck on problem 14, stepping away lets your brain sort through the logic while you commute or sleep. Cramming takes that breathing room away, turns studying into pure stress, and leaves you blanking on the concepts when test day shows up.

The Commuter Schedule: 40 Minutes, Gym, and Work

Unlike students living in Scott Hall or University Village who can roll out of bed ten minutes before lecture, I have a 30-40 minute commute to UNO each way. If I leave home ten minutes late, I get stuck in Omaha morning traffic and arrive stressed.

To keep my life together, I had to build a steady routine:

  • Early Morning Gym Sessions: Waking up early enough to hit the gym, shower, and drive to campus before rush hour gets bad. Hitting the gym gives me real energy and resets my posture from sitting at screens all day.
  • Balancing a Job: Working shifts on top of 15 credit hours means free time doesn't happen by accident. Every hour between classes on campus has to be used well.
  • Getting Over Social Hesitation: As a commuter who drives home after class, starting conversations with new people wasn't easy. It's tempting to just put headphones on and walk straight to your car, but I learned that staying isolated really hurts your college experience.

Success in Computer Science II (CSCI 1620)

Heading into Computer Science II (CSCI 1620), I honestly doubted myself a bit. I hadn't touched Python in about a year, and 1620 is a core course in the College of IS&T that moves fast into object-oriented programming, data structures, and algorithm efficiency.

Instead of repeating my Calculus mistake, I started practicing right away:

  • I locked in on studying from day one, reviewing syntax and building small test scripts right after class.
  • Instead of just reading lecture slides, I actively coded along in my IDE and broke functions on purpose to see the error messages.
  • I treated weekly programming labs as a chance to actually learn the material instead of just chores to finish.

The result? I did great in CSCI 1620. Locking in on studying works, and practicing what you learn in class makes the concepts stick.

Where I Learned This (Lesson 8) Source: Personal Experience & Class Assignments

Specific Source: Computer Science II (CSCI 1620).
What it taught me: Practicing what you learn in class gave me a much deeper understanding of the material, so I could do well on tests and labs and actually use it in real software problems.

Why This Matters Doing the Work vs. Just Reading About It

In technical fields like computer science, reading about code isn't the same thing as writing code. When you practice through labs and small experiments, you build muscle memory and learn how to debug errors in a way slides alone can't teach you. Getting into that hands-on habit gave me the real technical confidence that helped me stand out later in internship interviews.

Reflect on Your Own Honest Self-Check for Incoming Students

"What's your biggest high school study habit that you know won't hold up once college speeds up?"

In high school, teachers remind you about due dates every day and keep your work structured for you. In college, syllabi are posted once, and nobody is going to tap you on the shoulder to tell you a test is three days away.
