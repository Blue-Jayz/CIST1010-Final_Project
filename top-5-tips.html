Top 5 Tips: The First-Year Survival Core

Step 4 in Class Walkthrough • My 5 biggest takeaways, what happened to me, where I learned them, and why they matter.

If you only remember five things from this whole guide, make it these five. Each tip breaks down what happened to me, the source I learned it from, and why it actually matters for your first year.

Tip #1

Get work done early so you have more time for yourself and friends.

What happened to me: I thought I could knock out a 30-question Calculus problem set late one night after work; I stayed up past 2:00 AM, felt like a zombie for three days, and realized that doing 5 questions a day over a week would've only taken 25 minutes an afternoon.
Where I Learned This Personal Experience

My Calculus homework all-nighter in my first semester (MATH1930-856: Calculus For The Managerial, Life, And Social Sciences). It taught me that cramming cost me sleep when the assignment had plenty of time to spread out.

Why This Matters Less Stress & Real Free Time

Finishing assignments early cuts out that constant background stress of knowing something is due soon. When you hang out with friends or head to the gym knowing your work is already done, you can actually relax without deadlines hanging over your head.

Tip #2

Don't fall behind. A grade is hard to bring back up once it drops.

What happened to me: Walking into CSCI 1620 after a full year without writing Python, I knew right away that skipping even one lab or lecture would leave me totally lost on harder object-oriented coding assignments later.
Where I Learned This Personal Experience & Class

Computer Science II (CSCI 1620). Practicing what you learn in class gave me a solid grasp of the material, so I could do well on tests and weekly labs instead of falling behind.

Why This Matters How College Grading Works

In high school, lots of small daily assignments give you room to cover up a rough test score. In college, a syllabus often puts 70% of your grade on just three exams, so digging out of an early hole is brutal. Staying ahead from week one is ten times easier than playing catch-up.

Tip #3

Find your study spot in the first couple of weeks.

What happened to me: I wasted good hours my first two weeks trying to study in loud campus food-court areas and at home before finally finding the quiet 3rd floor of Criss Library.
Where I Learned This Personal Experience

Trying several study spots during my first 1 to 2 weeks on campus. It taught me that I study best alone, in a quiet place, and on campus rather than at home.

Why This Matters Training Your Focus

Your brain picks up habits fast. Once you have one quiet, distraction-free study spot, sitting down there tells your brain it's time to lock in. It also saves you from wasting time and energy wandering around looking for a table every afternoon.

Tip #4

Ask your instructors for help. Don't be afraid.

What happened to me: I felt nervous about asking questions in big lectures until I started staying 2 minutes after class to ask quick questions, and I realized my instructors are laid-back, helpful, and genuinely want students to learn.
Where I Learned This Class Instruction

CIST 1010 (College and Career Success). It taught me not to be afraid of my instructors; it's not like they're really all that scary to begin with, and they genuinely want you to understand the topics you're reviewing.

Why This Matters Building Good Relationships

Instructors are some of the best people to have in your corner. One 10-minute chat during office hours can clear up a topic you'd otherwise be stuck on for hours. Plus, professors are the ones who hire teaching assistants, pass along internship leads, and write recommendation letters.

Tip #5

Put your phone away while you study.

What happened to me: Constant buzzes and notifications kept pulling my focus off tough homework until I turned on a custom Study Mode that only lets calls and texts from immediate family through.
Where I Learned This Peer Recommendation

A friend (they'd rather stay anonymous). It taught me how to cut out phone distractions and stay locked in on my assignments.

Why This Matters Deep Focus & Saving Time

Every notification breaks your train of thought and slows down what you're working on. Putting your phone out of reach lets you lock in and actually understand what you're reading. Work that used to drag on for four distracted hours gets finished in 90 focused minutes.

Final Reflection: How My First Semester Changed My Habits

Rubric Requirement Taking Ownership of My Time

Looking back at my jump from high school to UNO, my biggest personal growth didn't come from memorizing Python syntax or solving Calculus problems; it came from learning how to manage my time and energy.

When you're a commuter who works a job and stays consistent at the gym, you can't afford to just wing your week. My midnight Calculus all-nighter was a rough wake-up call, and honestly, it was the one I needed. It pushed me to drop the last-minute cramming mindset and build real systems: a color-coded Google Calendar, phone Focus Modes, daily practice in CSCI 1620, and a quiet desk on the 3rd floor of Criss Library.

Just as important was growing outside the classroom. Getting out of my shell to join NULLify and show up to the UNO Career Fair led to real momentum, including multiple interviews and one with Union Pacific Railroad.

College isn't about being superhuman; it's about setting boundaries, protecting your sleep, and having the discipline to get things done early so you can actually enjoy your life. If you use even two or three of these habits, you won't just survive your first semester at UNO, you'll do really well.
