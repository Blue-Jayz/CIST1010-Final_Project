Study Smarter: Environment, Tools & Ethics

Step 3 in Class Walkthrough • Finding the right study spot and using smart tools the right way.

Studying for longer hours doesn't automatically get you better grades. What actually helps is studying in a quiet spot, using the right tools to save time, and following your instructor's rules.

1. Finding Your Spot: The 3rd Floor of Criss Library

During my first week or two on campus, I tried studying all over the place. I tried sitting in the Milo Bail Student Center, but all the talking and foot traffic near the food court kept breaking my focus. I tried studying at home in my bedroom too, where my bed, TV, and fridge were constant distractions.

Then I checked out UNO Criss Library and found the 3rd floor.

Unlike the 1st floor (which is louder and better for group work), the 3rd floor is a quiet study zone. It's nearly silent up there. Everybody around you is locked into reading, writing, or coding, and honestly that quiet peer pressure helps keep you on track too.

Trying out those different spots taught me three big things about how I learn:

  • I study best alone: Group study is fine for reviewing, but learning something tough for the first time takes quiet focus by yourself.
  • I need real quiet: White noise in my headphones or a silent floor lets me work through tough math and CS problems without extra noise getting in the way.
  • I need to study on campus: As a commuter, staying on campus draws a clean line between "work mode" at UNO and "rest time" at home. When I pack up my backpack and drive home, my homework is already done.

Criss Library 3rd Floor Tip: Head toward the north and east windows. The individual study desks have natural light, outlets right there for your laptop and phone, and a quiet view overlooking campus trees.

Where I Learned This (Lesson 4) Source: Personal Experience

Specific Source: Trying several study spots during my first 1 to 2 weeks at UNO.
What it taught me: I study best alone, in a quiet place, and on campus rather than at home.

Why This Matters Why Your Surroundings Matter

Your surroundings quietly tell your brain what mode to be in. If you try to do homework on your bed, your brain connects that spot with sleeping and relaxing, so it's way harder to concentrate. Having one go-to quiet spot on the 3rd floor of Criss Library trains your mind to lock in as soon as you sit down, saving energy and helping tough material click faster.

2. Modern Note-Taking: Note Cast & NotebookLM

In college lectures, instructors often move fast and cover a lot of slides in one 75-minute class. If you spend every second frantically typing down every word they say, you aren't actually listening or understanding the concepts.

To study more efficiently, I started using two tools in my routine:

Note Cast

I use Note Cast to transcribe lectures and make quick audio notes. It catches anything I might've missed during fast explanations, so I can focus on listening and understanding in class instead of typing nonstop.

NotebookLM

I use NotebookLM to make practice questions, summarize long readings, and organize study guides. It's especially helpful when I'm putting together printed reference sheets for tests that allow paper notes.

The main idea behind using these tools is simple: spend more time studying your notes instead of making them.

Critical Policy Check: Check the Syllabus First

Before using any lecture recording, transcription tool, or AI assistant, always check each instructor's policy in their course syllabus. Rules on AI and recording vary a lot across classes at UNO: some instructors encourage responsible AI use, while others completely ban recording devices or AI help. Never guess; read the syllabus and ask your instructor if you're unsure.

Where I Learned This (Lesson 7) Source: Outside Tools & Course Syllabi

Specific Source: Note Cast (notecast.net) and NotebookLM; each instructor's syllabus for recording and AI policies.
What it taught me: How to make notes in a much easier fashion, so I spend more time studying notes rather than making them. After all, AI is the future; we might as well use it responsibly now.

Why This Matters Using AI as a Tool, Not a Shortcut

AI is changing how tech and everyday work get done, so learning how to use it responsibly now prepares you for what's ahead instead of leaving you guessing. Still, these tools are meant to back up your thinking, not replace it. Letting software handle the busywork of organizing notes frees up your time to actually practice problems, test yourself, and understand the material.

Reflect on Your Own Are You Preparing to Study, or Actually Learning?

"Are you spending more time making your notes look pretty (color-coding pens, retyping slides) than actually practicing problems and testing what you remember?"

Retyping notes feels productive because your hands are moving, but it's actually one of the weakest ways to remember material.

Close your notes right now. On a blank piece of paper, can you explain the main concept from your last lecture in your own words without peeking? If you can't, pretty highlighting won't save you on test day; switch your time from decorating notes to active self-testing.
